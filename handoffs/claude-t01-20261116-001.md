## Abstract-level comparison notes (NOT manuscript-ready)

These connect works using abstracts only. They indicate what to look for when reading; none may enter the paper until the cited sections are read and the numbers checked.

- **A.** W002 (Denison et al.) studies whether trained-in gaming generalises to tampering under a constructed curriculum, whereas W001, W008 (EvilGenie) and W117 (ImpossibleBench) measure how often already-deployed models take shortcuts without such training. These answer different questions (generalisation under training versus propensity at test time) and their rates should not share a table column. W003 (RewardHackingAgents) and W005 (protocol validity) shift the unit from the agent to the evaluation protocol: they ask whether a reported score can be trusted at all.
- **B.** W034 (AgentDojo), W107 (InjecAgent) and W030 (ASB) are benchmark definitions; W045 (adaptive attacks) and W031 (firewalls or stronger benchmarks) argue from adaptive attacks and benchmark strength that static-benchmark defence results overstate protection. W012 (silent failures) reports that scoring by tool identity rather than arguments changed a measured attack rate substantially on the same traces. Together these bear directly on RQ2.
- **C.** W108 (CaMeL) offers single-agent guarantees; W016 (Can CaMeLs Talk?) claims those guarantees do not compose when agents call agents. W013 (multi-agent AI control) makes the parallel claim for monitoring: per-agent monitors weaken as an attack is split across agents. Both are tests of whether single-run assurances survive communication.
- **D.** W122 (AI Control) defines the red-team/blue-team protocol evaluation that W021 (evaluating control protocols), W123 (Ctrl-Z) and W019 (TraceGuard) build on; W103 (adaptive attacks on trusted monitors) and W021 test adaptive red teams against those protocols. D-category "safety" is measured against a prompted attack policy, which is a different source of behaviour from the endogenous gaming in A and the external attacker in B.
